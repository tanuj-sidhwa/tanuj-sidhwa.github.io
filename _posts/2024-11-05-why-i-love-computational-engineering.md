Sometimes, the projects you start for a course assignment end up teaching you something much bigger than the course itself.

When I began working on a job scheduling simulation for my *Principles of Industrial Engineering (ME207)* course at IIT Indore, the goal was fairly simple: compare different scheduling algorithms like FCFS, SPT, EDD, and others for a single-machine system. At that point, I saw it mostly as an industrial engineering problem — sequencing jobs, calculating lateness, and evaluating efficiency metrics.

But somewhere in between writing loops, debugging arrays at 2 AM, and trying to make terminal outputs look readable, the project slowly became my introduction to computational thinking.

This was one of the first times I genuinely started using libraries like NumPy and Pandas in a meaningful way. Before this, matrices mostly existed for me inside mathematics lectures and linear algebra notebooks. Suddenly, they became tools I could actually *use*. Processing times, due dates, completion times — everything naturally transformed into arrays and structured tables. Instead of manually handling data line-by-line, I learned how powerful it was to think in terms of vectorized operations and matrix-style manipulation.

Even something as simple as formatting outputs in the terminal became surprisingly satisfying. I remember spending a lot more time than necessary trying to organize the results neatly so the comparisons between algorithms actually felt intuitive to read. It was still just a terminal window — no fancy GUI, no visual dashboards — but seeing cleanly arranged scheduling tables generated entirely through code felt rewarding in a strangely personal way.

What fascinated me most was observing how different algorithms behaved under changing conditions. One method minimized flow time beautifully but performed poorly in lateness. Another prioritized deadlines while sacrificing throughput efficiency. There was no “perfect” scheduling rule, and that realization made the project feel much more real than a textbook exercise.

In hindsight, this project was less about scheduling algorithms and more about learning how engineering problems can be explored computationally. It introduced me to handling datasets, structuring simulations, automating analysis, and thinking systematically about performance metrics. Looking back now, I can see traces of the same mindset in many of the projects I work on today — whether it is CFD workflows, optimization frameworks, or multidisciplinary aerospace systems.

It is funny how small projects quietly shape your trajectory.

At the time, this was simply an assignment submission. But for me, it became one of those projects that marked the transition from *writing code for the sake of coding* to actually using computation as a way to understand systems.

And honestly, that shift changed a lot.
